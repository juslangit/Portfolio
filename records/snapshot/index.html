SnapshotProject record · built 2026-09-17

Snapshot

Snapshot is a photography game. You walk into a kampung yard on a bright morning with a camera in your hands, and a client has sent a brief: three photographs, each with one specific thing they want. Aperture, shutter speed and ISO do here exactly what they do on a real camera, and the pictures are marked the same way. The whole game is one trade — open the aperture and the background falls away but the frame fills with light; speed the shutter up to stop the hen mid-step and the light drains out again; buy it back with ISO and the picture goes grainy.

What the player sees

Screens

Every screenshot on this page was taken by a scene that drives the real game from outside and saves a frame, because in a photography game almost every question that matters is a visual one. The house that turned out to be a fantasy treehouse, the grass that read as green tiles lying on the dirt, the yard that looked like dusk and the review screen whose photograph had collapsed to nothing were all invisible in the code and obvious in a picture.

The yard

One kampung morning, and the only place in the game. The house is built from primitives - posts, a floor, a deep verandah, plank walls with a doorway left in them, a steep gable roof - because the only stilt house available to download turned out to be a fantasy treehouse in front of a painted backdrop. Everything that was downloaded is scaled by the height the thing actually is, which is how a well that arrived 1,203 units tall became a well 2.4 m tall.

What the aperture does

The camera is Godot's physical camera: the f-number, the shutter speed and the ISO drive the exposure and the depth of field in the engine rather than a lookup table faking the look. These two photographs are the same view at the same exposure, eight stops apart in aperture, so everything that differs between them is depth of field.

The brief and the report

A photograph cannot be right or wrong on its own - only against what somebody asked for. So the game gives you a client, and the report afterwards carries the measurement behind every mark.

Idea

Where it came from

On 2026-09-17 Luqman asked for a new Godot game and said he had no idea for one. Four were offered, each aimed at a different one of his strengths and each avoiding the ground his existing games already cover:

IdeaWhat it wasWhy not
Print FarmA 3D printing shop. Orient the model on the bed, add supports, choose a layer height, watch it print or fail.Recommended, because his day job teaching 3D printing is an unfair advantage nobody in that niche has. Not chosen.
SnapshotA photography game in one beautiful diorama, scored on composition.Chosen.
One RoomA single, properly modelled escape room.Smallest scope and the purest art showcase, but the least original.
KargoForklift physics in a warehouse, stacking pallets before the lorry leaves.The collapse comedy overlaps Kedai Runtuh too closely.

He then answered three shaping questions and picked: a kampung morning for the setting, client briefs as the structure, and a fully manual camera. Then: "dont ask question again, just do it. ask after finish."

The idea itself

A photograph cannot be right or wrong on its own. It is only right or wrong against what somebody asked for. So the game gives you a client.

Three clients, three shots each. A heritage trust wants the old house photographed properly and only asks that the light be right. A cafe wants three warm, close pictures with the background blurred. A magazine has had enough of blurred chickens and wants the movement stopped, and clean, with no grain. Each brief teaches one control of the camera and then asks for it under pressure.

The camera is real. Aperture, shutter speed and ISO run through the standard photographic formulas, Godot's physical camera attributes turn them into the rendered picture, and the same formulas mark the result. Every score carries the number it came from: "the cat at 2.8 m; sharp from 2.7 m to 2.9 m", "the hen moved 9.4 px during 1/30 s — a faster shutter would stop it".

Why this shape and not another

Why one diorama rather than several. Because the art is the gameplay. A photography game gets better as the scene gets better, so the right move is one place done to a standard worth photographing rather than five places roughed out. It is also the shape that lets Luqman own the result: the yard is his to replace, prop by prop, without touching a line of code.

Why client briefs rather than a shot list. A checklist of named shots was the alternative and would have been easier to score. Briefs were chosen because they carry a voice — the client's own words are the tutorial, and a sentence like "we like the frame it gives" teaches composition better than a rule printed on screen.

Why a fully manual camera rather than zoom-and-focus. This was the decision that made the game worth building. With only framing to score, the game is a test of taste. With the exposure triangle in it, there is a real puzzle with three competing controls and a correct answer the player can be led to — and what they learn is transferable to a camera they actually own.

What it is not

Not a walking simulator with a score attached, and not a photo-mode. The constraint is what makes it a game: you cannot have a soft background and a frozen hen and a clean picture all at once unless you find the light for it.

06-decisions.md · 10 sections

Decisions

D-001 — The camera is real, not a resemblance of one

2026-09-17. Aperture, shutter and ISO run through the standard photographic formulas, and Godot's CameraAttributesPhysical turns them into the rendered picture rather than a lookup table faking the look.

The alternative was to fake it: pick a nice-looking blur for "wide aperture" and score the player on whether they chose it. Cheaper, and nobody would notice.

Chosen against because the correspondence is the product. Anything the player learns here transfers to a camera they own, and the review screen can say "the cat at 2.8 m; sharp from 2.7 m to 2.9 m at f/2" — a sentence that is only worth printing if it is true. It also means the formulas can be checked against answers that exist outside the game, which dev/checks/_optics.gd does against published depth of field tables, hyperfocal charts and the sunny 16 rule.

D-002 — Exact stops internally, printed marks on the dial

2026-09-17. f/11 is stored as 11.3137 and 1/125 s as 1/128 s, and the dial prints "11" and "125".

Found by a failing check. With nominal values, closing the aperture one stop and halving the shutter did not cancel out — the arithmetic was off by a few hundredths and the exposure triangle, which is the entire game, did not quite add up. Real cameras work exactly this way: the markings are rounded, the values are powers of the square root of two. Doing the same made the trade exact and kept the camera reading like a camera.

D-003 — Linear tone mapping, on purpose

2026-09-17. The Environment uses TONE_MAPPER_LINEAR rather than Filmic or AgX.

A filmic curve looks better on a badly exposed frame — that is what it is for — and that is exactly the problem. The light meter in this game reads the recorded pixels and compares them with middle grey, so a curve that quietly rescues the highlights would make the meter lie, and a three-stop overexposure would look like an artistic choice. Linear means a blown sky really is blown and the meter tells the truth. The cost is a harsher-looking image, which is accepted.

D-004 — The light meter reads the pixels, and is centre-weighted

2026-09-17. Rather than bookkeeping a scene EV, the meter renders what the lens sees and averages its brightness, weighting the middle of the frame about eight times the corners.

Reading the pixels means the meter already knows about the sun, the shade under the house and wherever the player happened to point — no separate model of the scene to keep in sync. The weighting was added after a flat average was fooled by the sky: point at the house with sky above it and a flat meter called the house correct when it was two stops dark. That is the most common way a real photograph goes wrong, and a real camera answers it with centre-weighted metering, so this does too.

The metering happens in a 96×54 second view of the scene rather than on the window, because the viewfinder overlay is drawn on top of the window and its dark panels were making the morning read as dark.

D-005 — Motion blur is really rendered, not filtered

2026-09-17. Pressing the shutter renders the frame up to eight times, stepping everything that moves a fraction of the exposure between each, and averages the results.

A post-process blur was the alternative. Rejected because it cannot know what was moving: the washing sways, the hen paces, and a filter would smear the house as well. Rendering the exposure properly means the smear belongs to the thing that moved, which is the whole of the shutter-speed lesson.

It required everything that moves to be driven by a phase rather than by the clock — hence Mover — because something reading delta cannot be stepped by hand. The averaging is done with Image.blend_rect_mask and a 1/n alpha mask, a running mean entirely in C++; doing it per pixel in GDScript would be millions of calls per photograph.

D-006 — The house is built in code, not downloaded

2026-09-17. The only stilt house on Sketchfab turned out to be a stylised fantasy treehouse standing in front of a painted blue backdrop — the wrong building, in the wrong style, with scenery attached. It was downloaded, placed, photographed, and thrown away.

Replaced with about eighty lines that build a kampung house from boxes: posts, a floor, a deep verandah, plank walls with a doorway and shuttered openings, a steep gable roof, steps. It has the right proportions, the right roof pitch, and a doorway the player can stand in and shoot out of — which the model never would have.

The general lesson, and it applies to the next game too: a downloaded asset is only a shortcut if it is the right asset. Thirty lines of boxes with the right measurements beat a finished model of the wrong thing.

D-007 — Props are scaled by real height, never by a multiplier

2026-09-17. Each model is measured after loading and scaled so it stands the height the thing actually is: a well is 2.4 m, a hen 0.42 m, a kettle 0.26 m.

The well arrived 1,203 units tall. A hand-tuned multiplier would have fixed it and left a magic number nobody could check. Dividing by the real height leaves a number anybody can argue with, corrects an asset that was exported at any scale at all, and lets dev/checks/_scene.gd measure the result afterwards and fail if it is ten per cent out.

Scale is not cosmetic here: the judge marks how much of the frame a subject fills, so a well at twice its height is a well that can never be framed as the client asked.

D-008 — Client briefs, not a shot list

2026-09-17. Luqman's choice from three options. The client's own words are the tutorial: "we like the frame it gives" teaches composition better than a rule printed on screen, and each brief is built around one control of the camera so the lesson is never muddled.

D-009 — The sun does most of the lighting, not the sky

2026-09-17. The first calibration that exposed the yard correctly had the sky doing about three quarters of the work. It metered perfectly and looked terrible: turning the sun off changed almost nothing, the shadows were pale, the light had no direction, and "the morning light on it" was an instruction a player could not follow.

Rebalanced to roughly two to one in the sun's favour — 35,000 lux against an ambient fill of 0.084 — and dev/checks/_light.gd now fails if turning the sun off costs less than a stop. The sky is left at full brightness for the background, because dimming the sky itself to control the exposure had made the whole scene read as dusk.

D-010 — One yard, done properly

2026-09-17. No second location in v1. In a photography game the art is the gameplay, so one place worth photographing beats five roughed out — and it gives Luqman something he can improve prop by prop without touching code.

Looking at the game

Claude cannot see the game, so the art is reviewed through screenshots:

/Applications/Godot.app/Contents/MacOS/Godot --path . --resolution 1920x1080 dev/looks/_yard.tscn

That drives the real game, stands the photographer at fourteen viewpoints, and saves PNGs into dev/shots/ along with the metered exposure at each one. It is how the fantasy-treehouse house, the flat grass cards, the dark dusk lighting and the collapsed review-screen layout were all found — none of them were visible in the code.

Aim at subjects, do not guess angles. The first version of that harness set yaw and pitch by hand and put the well off the side of the frame. _aim() now asks the scene where a subject is and points at it.

Stand back from geometry. A viewpoint set inside the steps gets pushed out by the player's own collision capsule and the camera ends up aimed at nothing.

Recalibrating the light

The single most important number in the project is the pair SUN_LUX and AMBIENT_ENERGY in scripts/kampung.gd. They decide which camera settings expose the yard, which is what makes the dials mean anything.

Whenever the art changes — a wall repainted, the ground recoloured, the haze adjusted — the light needs recalibrating. dev/checks/_light.gd is what says so: it renders the yard at f/8, 1/125, ISO 100 (EV 13) and fails if the frame is more than two thirds of a stop from middle grey. dev/looks/_sweep.gd sweeps values to find the new pair.

Two traps found while doing it the first time:

  • The sweep is only trustworthy on its first sample. Changing a light or an Environment on a live scene and re-reading a third of a second later gives unstable numbers. Trust the first row, or re-run.
  • It is not only about brightness. The first calibration that exposed correctly had the sky doing three quarters of the work, so turning the sun off changed almost nothing, the shadows were pale and the light had no direction at all. _light now insists the sun is worth at least a stop on its own.
Swapping a prop

scripts/kampung.gd scales every downloaded model by the height it should be in real life, rather than by a multiplier. So to replace one:

  1. Drop the new mesh in assets/ and point MODELS at it.
  2. Leave HEIGHTS alone — it says what the thing is, not how the file was exported.
  3. Run _scene, which measures the result and fails if it is more than ten per cent out.

This is why the well works. It arrived from Sketchfab 1,203 units tall — a well the height of a small mountain — and dividing by the real 2.4 m fixed it with a number anybody can check.

Audio

The sfx tool downloads Freesound originals into assets/audio/freesound/. Godot cannot import a 24-bit WAV — it fails with "Format not supported for WAVE file (not PCM)" — so each one is converted with ffmpeg to 16-bit PCM and saved beside it as the file the game actually loads:

ffmpeg -y -i freesound/original.wav -acodec pcm_s16le -ar 44100 shutter.wav

The freesound/ folder holds a .gdignore so Godot stops trying to import the originals, while they stay in the repo as provenance.

The photograph pipeline

Worth understanding before touching camera_body.gd. Pressing the shutter:

  1. hides the viewfinder overlay, so it is not in the photograph;
  2. holds every Mover in the scene, so nothing reads the clock any more;
  3. renders the frame between one and eight times depending on shutter speed, stepping the movers a fraction of the exposure between each;
  4. averages those renders into one image — which is where motion blur comes from, and it is a real blur of real renders, not a filter;
  5. measures every subject in the scene: in frame, occluded, how much of the frame it fills, distance, what is behind it, whether the line of sight passed through a doorway;
  6. adds grain for the ISO that was chosen;
  7. hands the photograph and the measurements to the judge.

The averaging is done with Image.blend_rect_mask and a mask filled to 1/n alpha, which is a running mean done entirely in C++. Doing it with get_pixel in GDScript would be millions of calls per photograph.

  • optics.gd knows no scene tree at all. It is the photography, and it is the reason the game can be checked against a depth of field table.
  • camera_body.gd is the only part that can see the scene. It measures, and hands over a Judge.Reading — a plain bag of numbers.

A brief names its subject with a string — "cat", "laundry" — and the yard carries Subject nodes with matching ids. A typo there would produce a shot that can never be taken and always scores zero, with no hint why. So dev/checks/_scene.gd walks every shot in every brief and fails if it names something that is not in the yard.

07-references.md · 3 sections

References

The photography the game is built on

These are the formulas in scripts/optics.gd. They are standard, which is the point — the checks compare the game's answers with values from published tables.

Exposure value. EV = log2(N² / t), where N is the f-number and t the shutter time in seconds. Shutter is carried around as its denominator, the way it is written on a dial.

Correct exposure. A scene of brightness EV100 is correctly exposed when log2(N²/t) = EV100 + log2(ISO/100). Doubling the ISO is worth exactly one stop.

The sunny 16 rule. In full sun, f/16 at 1/ISO is correct — EV 15 at ISO 100. Checked directly.

Circle of confusion. 0.029 mm for a 35 mm frame: the blur spot small enough that a print looks sharp.

Hyperfocal distance. H = f²/(N·c) + f. Focus there and everything from H/2 to the horizon is acceptably sharp. Checked against the standard values — 35 mm at f/11 is about 3.87 m, 24 mm at f/16 about 1.26 m.

Depth of field. near = s(H−f)/(H+s−2f), far = s(H−f)/(H−s), infinite when s ≥ H. Checked against a full-frame table: a 50 mm lens at f/8 focused at 5 m is sharp from 3.43 m to 9.25 m.

Background blur. The blur circle the background lands on the sensor as: (f²/N) · |b−s| / (b(s−f)). Compared with the circle of confusion to decide whether a background has genuinely fallen away — the review screen prints it as a multiple, "42 times blurrier than sharp at f/2".

Middle grey. 0.18 in linear light. A camera's meter is built to render whatever it points at as this, which is why a photograph of snow comes out grey.

Field of view. 2·atan(12/f) degrees vertical on a 24 mm-tall frame, which is what makes a 24 mm lens and a 135 mm lens different photographs of the same thing.

Engine
  • CameraAttributesPhysical — exposure_aperture, exposure_shutter_speed, exposure_sensitivity, frustum_focal_length, frustum_focus_distance. Verified by experiment that depth of field really does render from these: the same view at f/1.4 and f/22, exposure-matched, differs by 0.18 mean pixel value (dev/looks/_dof.gd).
  • Physical light units must be switched on in project settings (rendering/lights_and_shadows/use_physical_light_units) before DirectionalLight3D.light_intensity_lux means anything.
  • Image.blend_rect_mask with a mask filled to 1/n alpha is a running mean in C++ — the trick that makes averaging eight 1080p renders per photograph affordable.

What the checks and the screenshots caught

Almost everything that was wrong was invisible in the code:

  • Nominal f-stops did not cancel. f/11 and 1/125 are rounded markings, not values. With them, a stop of aperture did not exactly cancel a stop of shutter — so the exposure triangle, which is the whole game, did not add up. Fixed by storing exact powers of √2 and printing the marks (D-002).
  • The house was a fantasy treehouse on a painted backdrop. Only visible in a screenshot. Thrown away and replaced with eighty lines of boxes that have the right proportions and a doorway you can stand in (D-006).
  • The well arrived 1,203 units tall. Which is why nothing is scaled by a multiplier any more (D-007).
  • The yard looked like dusk. Dimming the sky to control exposure was the cause. The sky went back to full brightness and the fill light was set separately (D-009).
  • The sky was doing three quarters of the lighting. Metered perfectly, looked flat, and turning the sun off changed nothing. Rebalanced two to one in the sun's favour, and _light now fails if the sun is worth less than a stop.
  • A flat light meter was fooled by the sky — the most common way a real photograph goes wrong. Made centre-weighted, like a real camera's.
  • The review screen collapsed. The photograph was a TextureRect told to fit its width, which gives it no minimum size, so it shrank to nothing and the panels never filled the screen. Only visible in a screenshot.
  • The grass was flat cards lying on the dirt with no silhouette. Now cones.
  • A dark band right across the house where the walls stopped level and the roof rose to the ridge. Closed with a fascia.
  • Screenshot viewpoints set by hand missed their subjects, and one stood inside the steps, where the player's own collision capsule shoved the camera somewhere else. The harness now asks the scene where a subject is and aims at it.

Where it stands

Playable end to end. The first shot the flow check takes scores 92 and the client says "They are using this one." A photograph of the cat at 85 mm and f/2 comes back with a genuinely soft background and a report that reads "the cat at 2.8 m; sharp from 2.7 m to 2.9 m" and "background 42.0 times blurrier than sharp at f/2".

Nobody has played it. That is M10 and the only thing that matters next.

Checks and looks

A check runs and ends in a verdict. A look takes screenshots for a person to judge. Both are scenes under res://dev/. Between them they caught nominal f-stops that refused to cancel against the shutter, a well a kilometre tall, a sky doing three quarters of the lighting, a light meter fooled by that sky, and a photograph that had shrunk to a white square in the corner of the review screen.
