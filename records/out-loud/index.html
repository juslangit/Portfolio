Out Loud

Out Loud is a Japanese class that runs in a browser tab and refuses to move on until you have said the phrase out loud. The teacher speaks first, using a Japanese voice already installed in macOS; the microphone then opens, Chrome turns what Luqman said into Japanese text, and the app compares it to what it asked for. Every lesson drills its phrases and then forces him to use them in a scene where the teacher plays the other person. It exists because Luqman asked to learn languages by speaking rather than by tapping, "like a real person teaching in class".

Why this project exists

Luqman wants to learn another language and was explicit about the method: the app should teach by speaking and make him speak back, the way a teacher in a classroom does. The apps that dominate this space teach by tapping — you match a word to a picture and never open your mouth, so the part that actually fails in a real conversation is the part that never gets practised.

The constraint that shaped everything: he has no paid LLM API key, which is the same reason the siti project is paused. A conversational AI tutor was therefore off the table on 2026-09-17. What is free and already on his machine is macOS speech synthesis (nine Japanese voices) and Chrome speech recognition, and those two together are enough for a genuinely spoken class as long as the lesson plan is written in advance.

What counts as "done"

v1 is done when Luqman can sit down, open it, be spoken to in Japanese, answer out loud, and be told honestly whether he was right — without typing anything and without spending anything. That is built. It is not verified: nobody has spoken to it yet, which is the single open item.

Known limits

  • Chrome only. Safari's speech recognition is unreliable, Firefox has none.
  • The listening half needs internet; Chrome transcribes the audio server-side.
  • Recognition is tuned for native speakers, so a beginner's accent can be transcribed badly and read as the app being unfair.
  • The teacher cannot improvise. It follows a written lesson plan, because there is no language model behind it.

01-idea.md · 6 sections

The idea

Problem

Luqman wants to learn a new language. The mainstream apps teach by tapping — matching words to pictures, arranging tiles into sentences — and it is possible to reach a high streak in one without ever having said a sentence aloud. The skill that fails first in a real conversation is the one those apps never exercise. His own words on 2026-09-17: the app should teach "through speaking", he has "to speak to learn", and it should be "like a real person teaching in class".

Proposed solution

A browser page that behaves like a classroom. The teacher speaks Japanese first, out loud. The phrase appears on screen with its reading, its sound spelled in English letters, and its meaning. The microphone then opens and Luqman repeats it. What he says is transcribed and compared to what was asked for, and he is told plainly whether he got it, got close, or said something else entirely. He does not advance until he has spoken. Each lesson drills its phrases and then puts them in a scene where the teacher plays a colleague, a waiter or a stall owner and will not continue until he answers.

Target users

SegmentNeedWhy they'd pick us
LuqmanLearn Japanese by speaking, at no costIt is the only part of the skill his existing options do not train
Anyone with a Mac and ChromeSpeaking practice without a tutor or a subscriptionNo account, no key, no fee — and the lesson content is a single editable file

Value / advantage

  • Speaking is not optional. There is no tap-only path through a lesson.
  • It costs nothing to run, forever, because both the voice and the transcription are features of software he already has.
  • The lesson content is one plain file, so he can add or rewrite lessons without touching any of the machinery.
  • The marking explains itself. Being told "you said turn right, I asked for turn left" teaches more than a red cross.

Deliberately not in v1

  • A conversational AI tutor. Rejected on cost grounds on 2026-09-17 — see 06-decisions.md.
  • Writing practice. Reading hiragana is not the same skill as speaking and would have diluted the one thing the app is for.
  • Any language other than Japanese. The machinery is language-agnostic; only the content file and two language tags would change.

Planning

Scope of v1 (built 2026-09-17)

In:

  • Five Japanese lessons: Greetings, Introducing Yourself, At the Restaurant, Finding Your Way, Shopping and Numbers.
  • Each lesson: 5–6 phrases drilled, then a scene of 6–9 turns using them.
  • Spoken teacher using a macOS Japanese voice, pickable and remembered.
  • Spoken answers marked, with the teacher replying out loud in Japanese.
  • A review round over whatever was missed, before the scene.
  • A report with the best score on each phrase, and a best-ever score per lesson kept in the browser.
  • Controls to interrupt: say it again, my turn now, skip this one, leave class.

Out:

  • Any language model. No free conversation, no answering invented questions.
  • Any account, server, database or payment.
  • Writing or reading drills.
  • Mobile. It is built to work on a phone-width screen but has not been tried on one, and iOS Safari does not support the recognition half.

M3 — Marking that survives Japanese

Goal: a correct spoken answer is marked correct, and an incorrect one is not, despite the recogniser writing kanji for kana and despite phrases that differ by a single character.

Done criteria:

  • done Katakana folds to hiragana before comparison
  • done Punctuation and spaces the recogniser invents are ignored
  • done Every accepted spelling of every phrase scores a pass against itself
  • done All five recogniser alternatives are checked, not just the first
  • done Saying a different phrase from the same lesson never passes
  • done An unfinished answer is distinguished from a wrong one

What it caught: the first version of the marking passed 右に曲がってください (turn right) at 90% when the answer was 左に曲がってください (turn left). Marked on text similarity alone, Luqman would have said the wrong direction and been told he was correct. Fixed by scoring every answer against the rest of the lesson too — see 06-decisions.md, D-003.

04-methods.md · 5 sections

Methods

Making a browser speak Japanese and listen back

Two browser features do the whole job and neither costs anything.

speechSynthesis reads text aloud using the voices installed in macOS. Checked on 2026-09-17, Luqman's Mac has nine Japanese voices — Kyoko, Eddy, Flo, Grandma, Grandpa, Reed, Rocko, Sandy, Shelley. Kyoko is the long-standing default and the clearest. getVoices() is populated asynchronously in Chrome, so the list must be read after the voiceschanged event, with a timeout as a safety net because that event occasionally never fires.

SpeechRecognition (as webkitSpeechRecognition in Chrome) turns speech into text. Set lang = 'ja-JP' and it returns Japanese. Two settings matter: maxAlternatives = 5 gives five guesses instead of one, which markedly improves the chance that the correct spelling is among them, and interimResults = true lets the screen show that it is hearing something while you are still talking.

The rule that matters: never run them at the same time. An open microphone during the teacher's line hears the teacher and marks the learner on her pronunciation. Every step in js/app.js waits for the previous one to finish.

Chrome's end event is unreliable. speechSynthesis sometimes never fires onend, which would freeze a lesson forever. The fix is to estimate how long a line should take from its length and the speech rate, and release the lock on a timeout if the event has not arrived.

Marking Japanese speech

Three problems, each needing its own answer.

The recogniser writes kanji for kana. Say こんにちは and Chrome may return 今日は. Identical sounds, different characters, and a naive comparison marks a perfect answer wrong. Every phrase therefore carries an accept list of the spellings that count as the same answer, and katakana is folded to hiragana (subtract 0x60 from the character code) before anything is compared.

Similar text is not similar meaning. 右に曲がってください and 左に曲がってください are 90% identical as text and opposite as instructions. Edit-distance similarity alone passes the wrong one. The answer is to score every attempt against every other phrase in the same lesson as well. If a different phrase fits better than the one asked for, that is what was said, and it is wrong however close the characters look. It also produces better teaching: the learner is told which phrase they said instead.

An unfinished answer is not a wrong one. ありがとうございます minus ございます is ありがとう — 50% similar, which reads as a failure, but it is the casual form of the right answer. When one normalised string is a prefix of the other, it is marked "close" and named as stopping short rather than as an error.

Testing an app whose input is a human voice

Claude has no microphone, so everything that can be checked without one is checked without one: tests/score.test.mjs runs 47 assertions over the marking and the lesson data with plain node, no browser. It feeds the scorer the text the recogniser would have produced, including the kanji forms and the deliberate near-misses.

Two of those checks failed the first time they ran, and both were real bugs that would only have surfaced mid-lesson with a microphone in Luqman's face. That is the argument for writing them: the parts of a voice app that can be tested without a voice should be, because the parts that cannot will absorb all the attention later.

The data checks matter as much as the logic ones — every scene answer must point at a phrase that exists, and no two phrases in a lesson may be close enough to be confused. Both hold a new lesson to the standard without anyone remembering to.

Serving it

Chrome only opens a microphone on https:// or localhost. Double-clicking index.html shows the page but the listening half silently fails, which looks like a bug in the app. ./run.sh serves the folder with python3 -m http.server on port 8722 and opens Chrome at it; ./stop.sh stops it. The app also detects window.isSecureContext and says so on screen rather than failing quietly.

Adding a lesson

Everything taught lives in js/lessons.js. Nothing else needs touching. A lesson is phrases plus a scene, and each you turn in the scene points at the phrase it expects by its position in the phrase list. Run the tests afterwards — they will catch a scene pointing at a phrase that does not exist, and two phrases too alike to be told apart.

06-decisions.md · 5 sections

Decisions

D-001 — Japanese first (2026-09-17)

Chose: Japanese, over Arabic, Mandarin and Korean. Why: Luqman picked it when asked. It also happens to be the best-supported of the four for this approach — nine Japanese voices already on his Mac, and mature ja-JP recognition in Chrome. Mandarin would have been the hardest, because the thing that most needs marking is tone and the recogniser does not expose it. Consequence: none structural. The machinery is language-agnostic; a second language is a new content file plus two language tags.

D-002 — A scripted teacher, not a language model (2026-09-17)

Chose: the free browser speech features with a written lesson plan, over an LLM-powered conversational tutor. Why: Luqman has no paid LLM API key — the same constraint that paused siti on 2026-09-13. He was given the choice explicitly, with the trade-off stated: free and scripted, working today, but unable to improvise; or a real conversation that costs money per lesson and cannot be built until a key exists. He chose free and scripted. What it costs: the teacher cannot answer a question Luqman invents or hold a conversation off the lesson plan. Within a lesson it is convincing, because the scenes are written as real exchanges and it genuinely will not continue until he speaks. Revisit when: a paid key exists. The drill and the marking stay as they are; an LLM would be a third mode alongside drill and scene, not a replacement.

D-003 — Mark against the whole lesson, not just the target phrase (2026-09-17)

Chose: score every spoken answer against all the other phrases in the lesson as well as the one asked for, and reject it if another fits better. Why: the first version of the marking used edit-distance similarity against the target alone. A test comparing every phrase against every other phrase found that 右に曲がってください (turn right) scored 90% when the answer was 左に曲がってください (turn left) — one character different in ten. Luqman would have said the wrong direction and been told he was correct, which is worse than no feedback at all, because he would have learnt the error. Rejected alternative: raising the pass mark above 90%. That would have made every longer phrase nearly impossible to pass, punishing real answers to fix a problem that is not about strictness but about meaning. Bonus: the app can now say "you said turn right, I asked for turn left", which teaches the distinction instead of just marking it wrong.

D-004 — Three tries, then move on (2026-09-17)

Chose: three attempts per phrase in the drill, two in the scene, then the teacher supplies the answer and continues. Missed phrases return in a review round before the scene. Why: being trapped on one phrase is how a learner quits. A teacher moves the class on and comes back to it, which is what the review round is. Open: whether three is the right number. Only Luqman using it can say, and it is a one-line change.

D-005 — Filed under web, named "Out Loud" (2026-09-17)

Chose: ~/Desktop/project/web/out-loud/. Why: it is a browser page with no server and no AI service, so web fits better than ai. The name is language-neutral on purpose — if a second language is added the name still describes it, where a Japanese-specific name would not. Luqman was told the choice and can overrule it.

07-references.md · 3 sections

References

The two browser features this is built on

  • Web Speech API — speech synthesis https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechSynthesis The teacher's voice. Note that getVoices() is asynchronous in Chrome and the voiceschanged event is not guaranteed to fire.
  • Web Speech API — speech recognition https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition The teacher's ears. Chrome only, exposed as webkitSpeechRecognition, and it requires a secure origin plus an internet connection.
  • Browser support table https://caniuse.com/speech-recognition Worth rechecking before assuming Safari still cannot be relied on.

Checking the Japanese

The lesson content was written to be ordinary polite Japanese, the register a learner should start in. Two things worth verifying with a speaker if the content grows:

  • The accept lists are guesses about what Chrome will transcribe. They are supposed to be corrected from real use — if a phrase keeps failing while Luqman is sure he said it right, whatever the app reports hearing should be added to that phrase's list.
  • The scenes use ordinary shop and office exchanges (いらっしゃいませ、ご注文は、 では、また). They are natural but not exhaustive.
